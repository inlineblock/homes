# Interiors, storage and everyday access

Use this guide with [the home design standard](../home-design-standard.md) before fixing exterior dimensions. It defines repository design decisions and review evidence, not a legal definition of a bedroom or a universal building code. A closet is mandatory for bedrooms in **our concepts**; its legal status depends on the jurisdiction. Identify the household, possessions, mobility needs, climate and maintenance expectations first. Do not equate a bigger house, a walk-in closet or more expensive finishes with a better plan.

Every selection needs a disposition: **included**, **deliberately omitted with reason**, **not applicable**, or **unresolved with owner and next action**. Unresolved functional omissions block a “reviewed” claim. Do not hide missing provisions behind attractive renderings.

## Tier choices, with a common functional floor

These are project profiles, not market statistics or regulatory classifications. Accessibility and basic storage belong at every tier. An expensive home may intentionally use a compact, restrained interior.

| Profile | Interior and storage priorities | Spend selectively on |
|---|---|---|
| Comfort / economical | Efficient furnished rooms; reachable bedroom wardrobes; entry coat and shoe storage; linen and cleaning storage; serviceable standard doors; useful task lighting | Better layout, daylight control, durable contact surfaces and easy maintenance before decorative complexity |
| Mid | Separate drop zone; more pantry and laundry capacity; solid-feeling hardware; flexible study/guest space; coordinated lighting and finishes | Acoustic bedroom doors, adjustable storage, robust drawer mechanisms and replaceable finish systems |
| Premium | Generous arrival; primary dressing capacity; secondary bedrooms still fully equipped; separate work/quiet zone; integrated millwork; layered lighting; explicit service storage | Refined joints, consistent reveals, discreet controls, integrated appliances and complete bathroom program |
| Custom / estate | Household-specific wardrobes, collections, staff or multigenerational needs, specialist hobby rooms and maintenance circulation when actually needed | Bespoke modules, acoustic assemblies, concealed service access, automation with manual fallback and future adaptation |

Premium bathroom decisions—including dual vanities, separate shower and tub, and an enclosed toilet room—must be resolved using [kitchen, bath and household support tiers](../research/kitchen-bath-tiers.md) and the home's program. They do not justify squeezing the dressing route, laundry, secondary bedrooms or foyer. Use [home program tiers](../research/home-program-tiers.md) for the broader household program.

## Decision cards

Each card states when to choose the feature, its interfaces, a library route and evidence to retain. Dimensions below are **starting design targets** where explicitly stated; measure the actual furniture and operating envelopes. They are not promises of regulatory compliance.

### Spatial placement: compose the room before placing assets

Every substantial fixture or furniture group needs a deliberate relationship to a wall, window, architectural axis or useful activity zone. Do not distribute objects through spare floor area simply because their bounding boxes do not collide. Evaluate the spaces between them: occupied approach, circulation, cleaning, reach and sightlines. Reject unexplained corridors behind beds, isolated tubs, stranded cabinets and narrow leftover slots beside showers. A deliberate kitchen island, conversation group or sculptural freestanding fixture can sit away from walls; its position must improve the room's use and composition.

For a freestanding tub, select a coherent bathing zone: for example aligned with a wall/window or deliberately centered in a properly sized wet room. Coordinate tub orientation, filler reach and placement, towel/soap ledge, entry/exit footing, shower access and splash separation. A tub placed near a wall still needs the selected product's installation and cleaning space; never solve visual awkwardness by jamming a freestanding shell against the wall. Avoid tiny uncleanable gaps, a filler stranded in a pinch point, or overlap with the shower's dry approach. If the room cannot support a convincing freestanding arrangement, compare an appropriate back-to-wall or built-in tub instead of forcing the original asset to fit.

Review the negative space in both a furnished plan and an eye-level view. Record why an object is freestanding where applicable and the actual gaps/operating zones; an attractive render does not make accidental placement intentional.

### 1. Arrival, foyer and mudroom

**Choose:** Every home needs an intelligible arrival sequence. Add a separate mudroom for frequent garage entry, wet boots, outdoor equipment, children or pets. A compact home can combine these functions without requiring a formal foyer. AARP's foyer guidance emphasizes an uncluttered transition and storage for carried and worn items. [AARP HomeFit: foyer](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/housing/info-2020/homefit-guide-video-foyer/)

**Reserve:** Show the parked-car-to-door route, sheltered pause, door swing, guest standing place, coat hanging, shoe/boot zone, bag shelf, keys/mail and a seat when useful. Keep the seat and open wardrobe doors out of the route. Make the public living destination visible or intuitively discoverable. A long corridor requires a recorded reason and relief through light, width, view or useful adjoining space; avoid 17 feet between blank walls as an accidental leftover.

**Library / proof:** Start with `cabinetry/oak-wardrobe-2ft/v001` for suitable hanging bays, not as a one-size-fits-all mudroom. Bench/cubby, boot tray and key shelf collections are gaps to publish when adopted. Render from just outside and just inside the front door at eye level; show two people arriving, an open door and ordinary stored items in the plan review.

### 2. Bedrooms and clothes storage

**Choose:** Every labeled bedroom needs its own usable clothes-storage provision. Select reach-in, freestanding wardrobe or walk-in by capacity and access, not tier alone. A reach-in can use less circulation area than a narrow walk-in. Primary suites usually need capacity for two people's different hanging and folded items; secondary rooms still need genuine storage.

**Reserve:** Inventory long-hang, short-hang, shelves, drawers, shoes, hamper, luggage and seasonal overflow. State net internal dimensions and rail lengths; a nominal 2-foot exterior depth does not guarantee 24 inches of usable internal hanging depth. Place the actual bed, bedside surfaces, dresser, curtains and closet door swings. Record clear widths at bed sides/end, dresser fronts and wardrobe fronts with drawers open. Check a route to each sleeping position and the window without climbing over furniture. Do not count inaccessible high shelves as daily storage.

**Bed placement:** In this collection, place the headboard against a deliberate solid wall or fitted headboard assembly, allowing a small documented trim/cleaning gap. Do not center a bed in a room and leave an accidental corridor behind it. Measure the evaluated headboard back, not the asset origin. Align bedside tables with the pillow/headboard zone, and check glazing, curtains, switches and access on both sides. A freestanding bed requires an explicit design reason accepted in the brief; it is not the default for a large bedroom.

**Premium walk-ins:** Design a dressing room around its actual contents. Include a considered mix of double-hang and long-hang, drawers/folded storage, shoes, adjustable shelving, hamper, full-length mirror and integrated lighting; record household-specific exceptions. Open-front systems can avoid a forest of swinging wardrobe leaves, but still need dust/maintenance and visual-order decisions. Measure usable rail/shelf capacity and drawer/hamper operation with a person standing there. Do not call a narrow residual gap a premium aisle because cabinets fit when closed. Add a bench or island only if dressing, two-person use and access remain comfortable; more furniture is not automatically better. Include a dedicated interior view and storage elevation/plan showing the different functions.

**Library / proof:** Link the existing two-foot wardrobe bays at their real scale, with filler/end conditions and hardware included. Reconfigure repeated internals through a new version or sibling asset; do not stretch doors and handles. Publish long-hang, shelving and drawer variants when needed. Include a furnished plan, wardrobe elevation and a storage capacity schedule for **each** bedroom. Daylight, ventilation and emergency escape requirements remain separate local checks.

### 3. Linen, cleaning and household overflow

**Choose:** Always assign locations for bedding, bath towels, cleaning equipment and general overflow. Increase capacity for guests, children, sports, holiday decorations and seasonal climates.

**Reserve:** Put linen near bedrooms/baths; tall broom/vacuum storage near cleaning routes; protected cleaning-product storage suited to the household; luggage and bulky seasonal items in reachable general storage. Include vacuum charging, ironing equipment, spare bedding, pet supplies and a place for recycling before outdoor collection. Avoid using equipment-service space or a parked-car aisle as a storage cupboard. AARP's room-by-room guide explicitly treats closets, laundry/broom storage and garage storage as distinct household functions. [AARP HomeFit room tour](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/housing/info-2020/homefit-guide-video-tour.html)

**Library / proof:** A wardrobe bay is not automatically a broom or linen cabinet. Clone its construction approach into correctly dimensioned tall-cleaning, adjustable-shelf and linen modules, publish them, and instantiate those versions. Record contents and net capacity by location; “storage somewhere in garage” fails review.

### 4. Laundry and utility use

**Choose:** Locate laundry by the household's clothes route and noise tolerance. Side-by-side equipment can support a folding counter; stacked equipment saves footprint but requires a real reach check. A premium laundry may add sorting, hanging, a sink and enclosed supplies; a second laundry is a program choice.

**Reserve:** Show equipment doors fully open, the operator and basket, sorting/folding space, wet-item hanging and an unobstructed exit. Resolve supplies, drain/vent or condensate interfaces, shutoffs, leak-management concept, service removal and vibration separation. Equipment installation documents govern actual recesses and ventilation. Do not assume a closet with decorative appliance fronts is resolved laundry.

**Library / proof:** Washer/dryer, hamper, laundry sink and drying-rail collections must be searched before use; if absent, publish generic scale-accurate concepts and label product installation unverified. Test bed-to-hamper-to-machine-to-fold-to-storage movement. Show both machines open and the route past them.

### 5. Living, dining and flexible rooms

**Choose:** Size social rooms around actual occupancy and furniture, including occasional guests. Provide a work/quiet place when the household needs calls or study. A guest/office combination must work in both modes. Children's rooms need changeable storage and floor activity space; do not permanently optimize around one age.

**Reserve:** Dining chair pullback, passage behind occupied chairs, sofa approach, circulation to outdoors, side tables, power and glare-controlled screens. Keep the main route out of the television sightline where practical. For flexible rooms, test the open sofa bed or guest bed, luggage, wardrobe and desk use separately. A future sleeping room must later satisfy its applicable sleeping-room checks; a desk rendering is not that evidence.

**Library / proof:** Reuse existing furniture at its documented scale. Missing sofa, side table, desk, shelving and guest-bed families should become original shared collections when repeated. Distinguish permanent partitions from furniture so later adaptation is honest. Save both furniture layouts and their clearance annotations.

**Game rooms, when included:** Choose the intended table size and cue length before fixing the room. Test full shot clearance and player stance on every side against walls, columns, seating and stair routes; a pool table fitting inside a room is not proof it can be played. Record equipment assumptions, table-light position, glare, delivery/assembly route and heating/cooling and acoustic provisions. A short-cue compromise needs an explicit decision, not a hidden clearance reduction. Apply the same activity-specific check to other selected games; do not invent a universal room dimension.

### 6. Doors, cabinets and hardware

**Choose:** Prefer lever operation for everyday hinged doors; select knobs, bar pulls, edge pulls or flush pulls for the specific cabinet/door action. Pocket doors can recover swing space but require pocket depth, wall coordination, accessible edge retrieval and maintenance access. Sliding barn-style doors do not automatically provide bathroom or bedroom acoustic privacy.

**Bathroom door schedule:** Decide each opening separately: hinged inward, hinged outward, or pocket, with a stated reason, hinge side and destination of the open leaf. An inward leaf must leave a usable standing place and avoid the toilet, vanity, tub, shower, towels and storage fronts. An outward leaf must not sweep a hall or another doorway into conflict. A pocket needs an actual unobstructed cavity, full travel, retrieval pull, privacy latch and service access, coordinated with plumbing, switches and cabinet fixing. Barn-style doors are disfavored for this user's bathrooms; use only by deliberate request with privacy limitations stated. Show closed, intermediate and fully open states in review; a decorative 55-degree leaf is not proof of operation.

**Toilet compartments and drawings:** Size the enclosure for comfortable use, cleaning and the chosen door, without leaving purposeless extra floor area. Compare the actual selected toilet and approach before reducing the room. Draw clear enclosure boundaries, a recognizable tank/bowl, the clear inside dimensions, and an unambiguous swing arc or pocket travel direction. A pocket must not look like a window. Label the WC as part of its ensuite, not an additional full bathroom. Check the plan at its published viewing size; text labels alone cannot rescue ambiguous geometry.

**Reserve:** Door leaf, jamb, stops, opposite-face hardware, latch, strike, wall return and opening arc; cabinet hinge/drawer envelopes; hand space at pulls. Flush and edge pulls require a usability check, especially with reduced dexterity. The Access Board identifies lever and U-shaped hardware as suitable forms under its operable-parts guidance; this is useful design context, not a claim that ADA applies to every private house. [Access Board: operable parts](https://www.access-board.gov/ada/guides/chapter-3-operable-parts/)

**Library / proof:** Current `library/hardware/` includes round knob, bar pull, edge pull, door lever and flush pull families in matte black, brushed steel and satin bronze, all `v001`. Link the intended finish and obey mounting origins. The door-lever asset models one face, not a complete lockset. The flush pull needs the specified host recess. Verify every rendered cabinet/door has an intentional means of operation; concealed hardware is a designed detail, not permission to omit it.

### 7. Light, daylight, privacy and quiet

**Choose:** For this collection's modern houses, recessed lighting is the default ambient starting point, with task lighting and selective decorative showpieces. Do not fill every ceiling with pendants. Add focused work, reading and mirror light; use a dining pendant or sculptural fixture only where it supports the composition and function.

**Reserve:** Recess depth and service access above ceilings; fixture alignment with furniture and cabinet fronts; switch/dimmer locations at arrivals; bedside controls; night routes; wall space for art. Review glare from lying in bed, sitting at dining and watching screens. Daylight needs a privacy and shading strategy, particularly in glazed bedrooms. Show curtain stacks, shade pockets, operable-window conflicts and cleaning access. Window-covering performance is a separate selection rather than an assumed property of large glass. [DOE / PNNL: window coverings](https://basc.pnnl.gov/library/energy-efficient-window-coverings)

**Library / proof:** Reuse `fixtures/lighting-recessed-downlight-3in/v001`, `lighting-undercabinet-bar-4ft/v001`, `lighting-linear-pendant-4ft/v001` or `opal-globe-pendant/v001` where suitable. Check ceiling cutouts and housing clearance; these generic assets do not establish fire, wet-location or photometric ratings. Add curtain/shade and wall-washer variants to the library when needed. Separate bedrooms from noisy media, laundry and equipment; record partition/door acoustic intent and mechanical transfer paths. A rendering cannot verify sound isolation or illuminance.

### Bathroom windows: daylight with privacy

Decide what a person should experience at each fixture: pleasant daylight, a worthwhile private view, glare control and freedom from feeling exposed. Check eye levels and sightlines for a person standing or undressing, seated on the toilet, reclining in the tub and using the shower. Check plausible street, path, neighboring-window and garden viewpoints outside. An illuminated bathroom can expose silhouettes or views that daytime reflections conceal; evaluate day and night conditions rather than treating reflective glass or a decorative slat screen as proof of privacy.

Choose the opening for its specific position. Options include a high clear window for sky/tree light, obscured lower glazing with clear upper glass, a genuinely private courtyard outlook, or a reachable moisture-appropriate shade. Obscured glass can still reveal nearby silhouettes, and slat screens can admit oblique views. Do not apply universal frosting or universal floor-to-ceiling glazing: use clear glass where the view and privacy are intentional, and preserve useful daylight where screening is needed. For an illustrative site, record the assumed privacy boundary rather than claiming neighboring sightlines are verified.

Coordinate windows with vanity mirrors and lighting, tub alignment/filler, shower controls, WC privacy, wall storage and towel positions. Avoid windows that force awkward fixture placement or put a toilet on display. Locate opening controls and cleaning access where a person can reach them without climbing on fixtures. For shower/wet-zone openings, show the intended moisture-resistant frame, sill/drainage and privacy strategy as concept decisions; selected glazing safety, waterproofing, ventilation, product suitability and local requirements remain separate verification. An operable window does not replace the room's exhaust strategy.

Retain a furnished-plan sightline note and an eye-level view that explain the choice. When privacy depends on a screen, shade or glazing treatment, show its intended operating state and limitations; do not let Image Gen invent the privacy solution.

### 8. Adaptation, mobility and maintenance

**Choose:** Consider temporary injury, aging, visiting relatives, strollers and larger mobility aids at every tier. Decide whether daily living can occur on the entry level. If stairs are unavoidable, record an actual future lift strategy or the limitation; “future elevator” without shaft, pit/overhead, approach and structural coordination is unresolved.

**Reserve:** Continuous routes, real door clear openings, latch-side approach, turning and transfer space, reachable controls and adjustable storage. For an early design study, a 36-inch route and 60-inch turning circle can be useful reference overlays, but doors, alcoves, approach direction and user equipment require separate examination. Those dimensions alone do not establish accessibility. The Access Board explains clear floor space, turning configurations and overlap restrictions; use the correct scoped standard when applicable. [Access Board: route widths](https://www.access-board.gov/ada/guides/chapter-4-accessible-routes/) [Turning space](https://www.access-board.gov/ada/guides/chapter-3-clear-floor-or-ground-space-and-turning-space/) [Door approaches](https://www.access-board.gov/ada/guides/chapter-4-entrances-doors-and-gates/)

**Library / proof:** Use adjustable variants rather than scaling standard cabinets into distorted substitutes. Test a furnished route with the relevant user's equipment envelope. Show cleanable floor transitions, replaceable wear surfaces, accessible filters and valves, and the ability to remove appliances. Deep ledges, high glazing, elaborate wood slats and concealed drains all need a maintenance strategy. Durable and repairable can be premium qualities without visual extravagance.

## Required review record and contribution loop

For each room, record occupants/activity, furniture, storage contents/capacity, actual clearances in normal and open-door states, light/privacy strategy, service needs, pinned assets, bespoke exceptions and outstanding checks. Record dimensions in feet/inches for presentation and meters internally. Keep concept-geometry evidence separate from code, acoustic, lighting and product verification.

Search every relevant `library/**/asset.json` before authoring. The catalog examples above were inspected on 2026-09-17; check current manifests, native files and previews before adoption. An asset's presence is not proof of host suitability or production approval. Missing options described here are a backlog, not claims of existing geometry.

When a missing component will repeat, publish it with editable source, dimensions, mounting origin, front direction, operation envelope, material dependencies, rights and a real preview. Instantiate the published version in the home and record that dependency. For a suitable asset needing adaptation, preserve its adopted version; create a sibling or next version, explain the difference, and explicitly migrate the home. Verify relative links in a fresh native reopen. Copied generator functions and many identical local objects do not satisfy shared-library adoption.

Finally, review eye-level arrival, furnished bedroom/wardrobe operation, cooking and laundry use, night-to-bath travel, guest circulation, and occupied indoor/outdoor transitions. Resolve the failures before calling the concept reviewed.
